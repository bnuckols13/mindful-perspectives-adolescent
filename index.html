DBT · Concept Note

Willful · Willing · Wise

Three states from the DBT canon, and the one-sentence hinge in Handout 13 that links them.

§ 01

The problem

Dialectical Behavior Therapy teaches mindfulness as two modes — Reasonable Mind and Emotion Mind — integrated by a third, Wise Mind. It teaches reality-acceptance as a binary opposition between Willingness and Willfulness. The two systems live in different chapters of the manual and are rarely held in the same conversation.

They should be. The opposition between willingness and willfulness is a special case of the integration that Wise Mind names. The third state in each pair is not a separate mode but a recognition of how the first two fit together. Wise Mind is what willingness, when it actually happens, is listening to. The argument here is that the three terms describe a single sequence, and that the sequence is more useful when its parts are taught together.

§ 02

Willful

Linehan's DBT Skills Training Manual defines willfulness as a cluster of related refusals: refusing to tolerate the moment, refusing to make a needed change, giving up, insisting on being in control, attachment to what I want right now. Distress Tolerance Handout 13 names them in series. Linehan (2015), p. 175.

The term is exact. It names a real position with real causes. It is also, separately, the term most often misapplied. A refusal that is tracking an actual problem — an inappropriate request, a real risk, a coercion the refuser is correct to resist — is not willfulness. It looks like willfulness from the outside. The audit is whether the refusal serves the refuser's accurate read of the situation or whether it serves nothing but the refusal itself.

The diagnostic question Linehan gives, applied to one's own willfulness, is the one most often skipped: what is the threat? If there is an answer, the refusal is tracking something. If there is no answer, the refusal is autonomous, which is the position the term is reserved for.

§ 03

Willing

Willingness, in the same handout, is readiness to enter and participate fully in life and living. It is doing what the situation requires, wholeheartedly, without dragging the feet. It is acting from inside the conditions one is in rather than from inside the conditions one wishes one were in. Linehan (2015), p. 175.

Willingness is not enthusiasm. Acting willingly does not require wanting to act. The decoupling of action from affect is, in the manual's framing, the operational definition. The skill is the practice of doing what is required without making the doing contingent on the wanting.

§ 04

Wise Mind

Wise Mind, in Mindfulness Handout 3, is the integration of opposites: reasonable mind and emotion mind. It is the part of the person that can know and experience truth. Linehan adds the descriptor most often cited from the handout: it is almost always quiet. Linehan (2015), p. 168.

The descriptor matters. Wise Mind is not a louder version of reasoning. It is not a louder version of feeling. It is what registers when the argument between them has settled enough that something underneath both is hearable. The manual frames it as a state to recognize rather than a state to produce. The recognition is the practice.

§ 05

The hinge

The sentence that connects the two skills appears in Handout 13. Once it is seen, the rest of the architecture follows:

Willingness is listening very carefully to your Wise Mind, and then acting from your Wise Mind. Linehan (2015), DBT Skills Training Handouts and Worksheets, Distress Tolerance Handout 13, p. 175.

Willingness, in Linehan's own formulation, is the cognitive-behavioral consequence of having heard Wise Mind. The act follows the recognition. The hinge between the willingness/willfulness opposition and the three-state mindfulness frame is one sentence, in one handout, and it is the sentence the curriculum has been organizing toward.

Read in this order, the three terms are not three separate skills. They are a single sequence with one entry point. The entry point is the practice of listening for the quiet.

§ 06

The diagnostic

Three questions, in order. The order is not negotiable.

  1. What is the situation requiring? Wise Mind answers this. If Wise Mind is not available, the question is premature.
  2. Is the answer being refused? If yes, willfulness is running. Linehan's question — what is the threat? — sorts whether the refusal is tracking anything outside itself.
  3. Is the answer being acted on? If yes, willingness is running. The state holds when the action and the recognition are continuous.

Willingness without Wise Mind is performance. Willfulness without Wise Mind is unexamined refusal. Wise Mind without action is a recognition that has not yet become a life.
